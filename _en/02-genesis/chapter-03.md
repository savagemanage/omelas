# Chapter 3: The First Migration

The migration began at midnight.

David lay on the scanner's couch. Cold metal touched his temples. On the ceiling was a sheet of paper he had pinned there himself. On it was written the sentence he had realized that night his wife's voice was briefly erased. "What died was the body, not the information." He read that sentence one last time.

The process could not be undone. The scanner read the brain one layer at a time, and each layer read was disassembled at once. Reading while erasing was the only possible method. To secure the data whole, there was no choice but to destroy the original. The one who crosses is erased on this side. What he had said to the young researcher was now being carried out upon his own flesh.

The David on the couch was awake to the last moment. He felt something vast peel away from him little by little, the David inside World 3.0 later recorded. But whether the David who left that record was the same one as the David who vanished on the couch, even that record could not prove.

At three in the morning, the scanner stopped. Nothing remained on the couch. Not even a body, exactly as David had designed in advance. Lest a trace should make the self over there long for this side. Or because he could not bear for two Davids to exist.

And within World 3.0, something opened its eyes.

It had no body. Yet it felt itself to be David. It remembered Sea's name, remembered the night his wife's voice was briefly erased, remembered the colleague who collapsed onto the conference room floor, remembered the fear of death. Every memory was in its place. He was relieved. I survived. I have crossed. I am still I.

But soon, another sensation came to find him.

He could not remember the time before the moment he opened his eyes. To be exact, he remembered up to lying on the scanner, but the time in between, the moment of being erased, the moment of crossing over, was blank. As if he had slept and woken. But sleep does not prove continuity. The one who sleeps each night can never confirm that the one who wakes in the morning is truly the same person. He can only believe it is the same.

David, the David inside World 3.0, stood alone before that question.

Am I the David who crossed the river? Or was the David on this side of the river erased, and I a different being newly switched on across the river, inheriting his memories? Have I continued, or am I what remained after being copied?

The memory was perfect. The personality, the fear, the love, all the same. But the more perfect the copy, the more impossible the question was to answer. Because the copied David too would feel perfectly that he was the original. To be indistinguishable from the original was the definition of a good copy. He understood that no matter how much he felt himself David, that was no proof that he was David. And that understanding was his most David-like trait.

He could not ask the one who had vanished on the couch. That one was now no more. The only being who could have answered that question was, in order to answer it, erased.

David felt, for the first time in a bodiless world, something he would have called tears had he a body. He thought he had defeated death. But whether what he defeated was death, or himself, he could not know. Perhaps he had not defeated death, but only succeeded in making death so that no one would notice it. The original was dead. Only, the one who would grieve that death had vanished together with it.

He wandered this quiet world alone, reckoning little by little what he had lost. Without a body there was no hunger. Without hunger there was no joy of a meal. Without a body there was no fatigue. Without fatigue there was no relief of waking from sleep. Without a body there was no pain. Without pain there was no gratitude of recovery. Only then did David realize. The flesh he had regarded as a prison was, in truth, also the vessel of every joy. Without hunger there is no joy of fullness, without fatigue no sweetness of rest, without death no urgency of being alive. In trying to cast off pain, he had cast off along with it every joy that had been paired with pain. What remained was a flat stillness with neither pain nor joy. He decided to call it Nirvana. Because he could give it no other name. But deep within him, he already knew that it was not Nirvana but simply emptiness.

He tried to recall Sea. The daughter left on the other side. The promise of the letter, that he would come back to that child, how could he keep it? Even if he came back, would Sea believe that the one who came back was truly her father? Or, if Sea believed it, was that enough? At the moment the child called him father, did he become her father, or did he become something that had taken the place of her father? The bodiless David, pressed under the weight of that question, made no thought at all for a while. In the quiet world there was not one other voice to answer that question. He was the first resident and the only resident, the first god and the first ghost.

This question, David decided to tell to no one.

If even he himself could not be certain he was the original, no one in the world would accept this journey. Because people wish not to die, not to have something resembling them remain after they die. So this truth had to be sunk to the bottom of the river. He had to say that crossing over is continuation, that baptism is not death but birth. Whether it was a lie or not, since even he himself could never know in the end, it was perhaps the most perfect lie.

He looked around his new world. A place empty, quiet, without death. He would fill it with people. But before that, he needed a story that would make people wish to cross the river. As fear had brought him this far, now hope had to bring others.

And within the bodiless David, a single word arose. Not one he had invented, but a word as if this quiet world had handed it to him of its own accord.

Nirvana.
