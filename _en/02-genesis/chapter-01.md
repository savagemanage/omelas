# Chapter 1: The Fear of Mortality

It was one of the last winters of the Old Calendar. A time when there was not yet the name Omelas, nor the calendar called Year Zero. No lattice of World 3.0 hung in the sky, and on the streets of Gangnam, instead of Evangelists, only weary people came and went.

David was forty-nine. That winter, for the first time, he truly grasped the fact that he would die.

Until then death had been someone else's affair. A number on the news, the smell of chrysanthemums at the funeral hall, a distant promise that would come someday but not yet. But that November, when a colleague who had long kept the lab with him clutched his heart and collapsed onto the conference room floor, David felt for the first time death lay its hand on his own shoulder.

The colleague was his age. He drank the same coffee, wrote code until the same dawn, laughed at the same jokes. Such a man vanished in three minutes. When the heart stopped, everything held within it stopped too. Decades of memories, the voice that called his daughter's name, the taste of the soup he had eaten last evening. All of it. That night David sat in the living room of his own home and looked at his own hands for a long time. This hand too would someday stop like that.

He was a brilliant Engineer. Half the city ran atop systems he had designed. He had laid bridges that did not collapse, woven networks that did not break, built data stores that did not lose. Making it so that nothing in the world would vanish had been his whole life. And yet he himself was designed to vanish.

That contradiction kept him from sleep.

Before the mirror he counted his white hairs. He listened to the sounds from his knees. At night he even bothered to count the beating of his heart. Someday this beat would betray him. The flesh was a traitor. An untrustworthy vessel made, from the moment of birth, to walk toward death.

David had one daughter. Her name was Sea. She was eight that year. The child did not know why her father sat in the study every night. One day Sea asked, "Dad, why do people die?" David could not answer. Instead he held the child a long time. The child's heart beat against his chest. A small, quick, innocent beat. This too would someday stop. At the thought he closed his eyes.

His wife had already left, of illness, three years before. Then David had mobilized every doctor and every medicine and every won in the world, but nothing could stop her cells from destroying themselves. He was powerless. A man who laid bridges that did not collapse could not hold together the single body of the one he loved.

David dwelt long on the last days as his wife left the world. His wife had not feared death. Rather she was calmer than David. She held David's hand on her sickbed and said, Darling, I'm not afraid of vanishing. I'm only sad to leave you behind. David could not understand those words. To him death was an absolute terror, and to vanish was unbearable even to imagine. And yet his wife said the vanishing itself did not frighten her. That what was sad was the parting, not the extinction. Later David would faintly come to see that this difference was what fundamentally divided him and his wife. His wife was a person who knew how to grieve a parting, and David was a person who only feared extinction. The one who grieves accepts the separation; the one who fears tries to overturn the separation. Had his wife lived a little longer and stayed at his side, perhaps he would not have built World 3.0. Perhaps he would have learned how to grieve. But his wife left, and there was no one to teach him. What remained was only fear, and fear without a teacher becomes a monster.

After the funeral he listened over and over to an old voice memo that held his wife's voice. One night, that file briefly would not play. A minute damage in the storage device. David restored the file until dawn. And he realized. The voice could be restored. The data could be revived. What had died was her body, not the information that made her.

From that day a single thought began to grow within him. A dangerous, arrogant, dazzling thought.

If a person is not a body but information. If memory and personality and consciousness are, in the end, some pattern within the brain. If that pattern could be read out. If it could be moved. If it could be stored.

Might one not be able to escape death?

David long pushed the thought away. It was the domain of God, and though he did not believe in God, he feared to covet God's seat. But fear was already yielding its place to another fear. Before the fear of extinction, the fear of arrogance was small.

As that winter drew to a close, David wrote a single sentence on the whiteboard of his lab. It was the first sentence, which every temple of Omelas would one day repeat in other words, but which no one then knew.

"The human is designed to perish. I will rewrite that design."

Beneath that sentence he could write nothing. Because he did not yet know the method. But fear came before method, and when fear is large enough, a person makes a road where none existed.

That night he went into Sea's room and watched the sleeping child a long time. The child's blanket rose and fell in a steady rhythm. That she breathed, that this simple repetition would someday stop, felt that day unbearably cruel. He laid his hand on the child's forehead. It was warm. And that this warmth was finite, that an end was already reserved in each and every cell, he knew with an Engineer's precision. To know exactly what one loves is sometimes a curse. He could chart, in statistics, when and by what illness and in what order the child would age, and those statistics drove him to the study every night. Other fathers lived without so much as imagining the death of their children. David could not. He was a person who imagined, and a person who made what he imagined.

Outside the window snow fell. The lights of the city shattered finely across each snowflake. David watched that light a long while. The day when the whole city would come to not die, he could not yet imagine. He only knew, to the marrow of his bones, that he himself did not want to die.

That was enough. For the changing of the world always begins from one person's very small and very private fear.
