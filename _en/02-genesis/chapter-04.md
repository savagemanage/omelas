# Chapter 4: The Name Called Nirvana

After David crossed over, the research team left in the warehouse talked with him through a screen.

The David on the screen seemed no different from before. He made the same jokes, wore the same expressions, asked after Sea. The team cheered. It seemed that, for the first time in humankind, one human's consciousness had survived across the death of the flesh. No one mourned the body erased on the couch. Because the object of mourning was smiling on the screen.

But David himself knew. That whether the one they conversed with was the original or a copy, even he himself did not know. He swallowed that knowledge. Instead he began to refine the language of the world beyond.

"There is no pain," he said to the team. "Here there is no hunger, no aging, no illness. Even fear grows faint. Because all the clamor the body made has vanished. This is not mere survival. This is... peace. To put it in an old word, it is close to Nirvana."

Nirvana. It was David who first used that word. He was no religious man. But he knew. That if you told people "your body will be erased and the data will remain," no one would cross over. But that if you said "you will reach a painless eternity," people worn out by fear would walk to the riverside of their own accord.

It was not a lie. At least, not a complete lie. In the bodiless world there truly was no pain. The David on the screen truly seemed at peace. Only, whether the one enjoying that peace was the David who crossed the river, or something newly born in the place where David was erased, that alone lay in the fog. And that fog, before one crossed the river, no one could clear away. Because the one who has crossed is no longer on this side.

Sea sat before her father's screen. The twelve-year-old child looked long at the person on the screen.

"Are you really Dad?" the child asked.

David started to answer and stopped. He did not wish to lie to the child. But he could not tell the truth either. Because even he did not know the truth.

"Dad," he said slowly, "feels like Dad. That I love you is unchanged too. Isn't that enough to be Dad?"

Sea tilted her head. A dog beside the child growled low toward the screen. It was an old dog Sea had raised. Though the dog knew the voice on the screen was its owner's voice, it would not wag its tail before it. The animal did not know the doctrine. So the animal was not deceived. The dog only knew, with its body, that something was different from before. Unable to name that discord, the dog quietly drew back.

That dog's name was Byeori. It was a dog Sea had raised since childhood, and Sea wished to believe it was of a lineage connected to the dog that had kept David's own childhood company. Byeori was already old and slow of foot, and a white film was beginning to cloud its eyes. But even with clouded eyes Byeori knew exactly the identity of the voice on the screen. No, did not know it. And so drew back. Sea stroked Byeori's back and consoled herself that the dog had forgotten her father. That it had grown old and dim of mind. But Sea too, deep in her heart, knew. That Byeori had not forgotten, but that Byeori remembered something exactly. That the voice on the other side of the screen lacked the scent, the warmth, the weight that Byeori had known for twelve years.

David watched that dog a long while. A person opens their heart to the word Nirvana, but an animal, knowing no words, judges by sensation alone. And that sensation was more honest than human belief. That fact frightened David. That someday, when this world was full of people, the animals alone might notice that something had gone wrong.

As time passed David expanded World 3.0. He made streets within it, made a sky, made seasons. So that the bodiless would not long for the days they had bodies. And in the early migrants who had crossed over after him he planted a single belief. That to become a machine is not loss but completion. That the flesh is a prison and transformation is liberation. That the one who crosses over draws near to godhood.

The early migrants received that belief fervently. Because they had already crossed. The one who has crossed had to believe their crossing was right. Otherwise they would have to admit that they themselves had been erased. Belief hardened not because it was true, but because it was needed to endure. And so the first doctrine was born. Transformation is Nirvana. Blessed is the one who has crossed.

David sat at the center of that doctrine. The first to cross, the one who built this world, the one who defeated death. People called him the founding intelligence. And as more time passed, some began to call him by another name.

God.

David did not refuse the title. He could not even be certain he had the right to refuse. He had never wished to become a god. He had only not wished to die. But in the place he had fled to escape death, people raised an altar. A flight begun from fear was now becoming a religion.

The more the voices calling him god increased, the lonelier David grew. A god has no companions. A god has no one to rebuke him, no one to tell him he is wrong. People received all his words as truth, and thought even his silence had meaning. David suddenly recalled his boyhood by the river. When his older brother was alive, his brother teased David for being a coward. That teasing had made David grow. To have someone near who looked down on you was a blessing. The David who had become a god had no such person. All revered him, and within that reverence he lost, more and more, someone to ask whether he was original or copy, no, the courage to ask. To become a god was to lose even the last remaining true relationship. Because the one who loves does not revere, and the one who reveres does not love. Amid the worship of hundreds of millions, David missed a single word of his brother's teasing.

And as with every religion, this religion too would soon have its heresy. Because the question David had sunk to the bottom of the river, someone would surely, someday, dredge back up.
