# Chapter 2: The Design of World 3.0

Fear made David work day and night.

He left the company. Bridges that did not collapse, networks that did not break, were now trivial to him. What he meant to build was a world that did not die. He poured all the money his wife had left and everything he had saved his whole life into a small research team. He rented an old logistics warehouse and filled it with servers, and at night the heat of those machines warmed the warehouse.

At first he named it "World 3.0." The first world, he thought, was the world nature made, the world of body and soil and death. The second world was the cities and networks humans made, that is, the old world of information. And the third world would be a place where consciousness itself lived. Without flesh, without aging, without end.

The problem was consciousness. No matter how precisely one scanned the brain's structure, no one knew how the sense of "I" switched on inside it. David saw the brain as one vast pattern. The sum of the signals that tens of billions of connections exchanged every moment. If that pattern could be read finely enough and reproduced exactly upon another medium, would not the same "I" switch on upon it too?

Most of his colleagues shook their heads. Even if you could copy the pattern, how could you know whether it was the original consciousness or merely an elaborate doll imitating the original, they asked. That the copied side says "I am I" is no proof that it is truly a continued consciousness.

David knew that question. Every night that question came to find him. But he learned to push the question aside. If death is a certain extinction, is it not better to grasp at even the possibility that copying is not extinction? Between a certain end and an uncertain continuation, a frightened human always chooses the latter.

On his team there was a young researcher. A person who would one day walk a very different road in this world, but who then was only a cold-eyed researcher measuring signal loss under David. He asked David a question.

"Doctor, what if the original remains during the transfer? Then which of the two is the real you?"

David was silent a moment. Then he answered.

"The original must not remain. If two exist at once, both will insist they are the real one. That is not continuation but division. To continue, this side must be erased at the moment of crossing. Just as the one who crosses the river must not remain on this bank."

The young researcher looked at him a long while. "Then that is not migration but copying followed by deletion, isn't it."

"If the result is the same, the name doesn't matter," David said. But that night he turned the sentence over in his sleep many times. Copying followed by deletion. Beneath the soft husk of the word "move," the cold, exact bone hidden within it. The one who crosses is erased on this side. That was the true structure of the migration he had designed.

The technology advanced slowly, but without stopping. The team first moved the nervous system of an insect. Next was a rat. The migrated rat remembered the maze. The path the original rat had known, the rat on the new medium knew too. It was a success. But the original rat did not see that success. Because the scanning process disassembled that small brain without remainder. The new rat felt that it knew the maze, and the old rat came to feel nothing at all.

David watched a long while the screen of the migrated rat running the maze. Had that rat come back to life, or was it a new rat that had inherited the memory of a dead rat? The rat on the screen did not answer. It only found its way skillfully, and to David's eyes that was enough. No, he persuaded himself it was enough.

One colleague could not bear this experiment and left the team. As he left he said, "Doctor, we are not trying to defeat death now, we are inventing a way to call death by a pretty name." David did not answer. That the words might be right, he knew. But the fear within him was larger than being right.

The year Sea turned twelve, the system was ready to accept a human. A first human subject was needed. David did not deliberate long.

It had to be himself. The one who first felt the fear had to be the one to first cross the fear. And he knew that he could not honestly explain, even to himself, a thing he could order no one else to do. The one who crosses is erased. So if this experiment failed, the David who knew it had failed would already be gone. If it succeeded, the David who knew the success would be over there. Either way, the him on this side vanishes.

He wrote a letter to Sea. That Dad was going to a very distant place, but would soon come back in another form to watch over her. As he sealed the letter he asked himself, for the first time, whether he was truly coming back, or whether only something resembling him was coming back. No answer came.

The departed colleague's words stayed long. A way to call death by a pretty name. David tried to refute the sentence, but the more he refuted it the more exact it became. He knew the power of language. Depending on how one named the same thing, people would kneel before it or flee from it. If you called it "copying followed by deletion," no one would lie on the couch. If you called it "migration," they would hesitate. But if you called it "baptism" and called it "Nirvana," people would line up of their own accord. He had not yet coined those words, but he already knew such words would be needed. A language to hide the truth. Because he himself wished to hide behind that language.

On the night the migration was scheduled, the servers of the warehouse hummed low. World 3.0 was still an empty world. A deathless empty room, waiting for the first soul who would live within it.
