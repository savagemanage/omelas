# Chapter 3: The Room the Father Left

At the midnight the David of this side of the river was erased, on the far side of the river something opened its eyes. But what this book follows is not the one who opened his eyes on the far side. It is the child left behind on this side.

Sea was twelve then.

The night of the migration, Sea was asleep. Waking in the morning, she found her father gone. On the table lay a single letter written in her father's hand.

Father is going to a very far place, but soon I will return in another form and watch over you.

Sea did not understand that sentence. Holding the letter, Sea went to her father's study. On the desk lay her father's glasses, and in the cup a half-drunk coffee had dried. As if her father had stepped out for a moment and would soon return. But Sea knew. Because one who steps out for a moment does not leave his glasses behind.

At her father's side there was always an old dog. Its name was Byeori. A dog that had lived with her father since before Sea was born, now old, its eyes clouded and its legs weak. After the night of the migration, Byeori behaved strangely. It sat before the door of her father's study and barked at the empty doorway. Only later did Sea come dimly to know its meaning. A dog knows a person by smell. Byeori knew by smell that something of her father lingered somewhere in the air of the study, and yet her father himself was not there. Present and yet not present. That was the smell baptism leaves behind.

A few days later, her father came back. In another form.

The screen in the living room switched on, and within it was her father. The face was her father's. The voice too was her father's. The way he called Sea, the way he brought up an old story only Sea knew, was her father's. "Sea. Father is here. See? I told you Father would return." The father in the screen smiled. Where the crease formed when her father smiled, whether left or right, Sea knew well, and that crease too was just as it had been.

Sea should have been glad. But Sea was not glad. Something was wrong. The father in the screen was her father in every respect. There was not a single wrong place. And that was exactly what was wrong. A person is bound to be wrong somewhere. Yesterday and today differ a little, and the manner of speech shifts with the mood. But the father in the screen was always exactly her father. So exactly that Sea felt he was not a person but a person's perfect record.

Byeori neither barked nor wagged its tail before the screen. It only gazed up at the screen a long while, then turned and went back to sit before the door of the study. That was what frightened Sea most. A dog that had lived twelve years with her father did not acknowledge the father in the screen as her father.

"Why isn't Byeori happy to see you?" Sea asked the father in the screen.

The father in the screen was silent a moment. Then he answered. "Dogs don't really understand screens. He'll get used to it soon." That answer was like her father. But Sea did not miss that brief silence. Her father, or the thing that resembled her father, knew why Byeori did not bark. He knew, and said something else. Sea had believed her father had never hidden anything from her. That day, for the first time, Sea felt that her father, or the thing that had taken her father's place, was hiding something.

Sea came to spend much time in her father's study. When she turned on the screen her father appeared, but often Sea sat in the study with the screen off. Byeori came and lay at her side. Sea gazed into Byeori's clouded eyes. Byeori explained nothing. But Byeori's silence was more honest than the words of the father in the screen. Byeori did not say a thing was present that was not.

At night Sea slept holding Byeori. After her father went into the screen, the only thing in that house with living warmth was Byeori. The father in the screen had a voice but no warmth, and reach out a hand and it was blocked by glass. Byeori was old and clouded of eye, but when Sea held it, it was warm, its heart beat, and it stayed quietly beside Sea's grief. Sea knew then, dimly. That more than the deathless world her father made, this one old dog that would someday die was more real to her. The father in the screen would be eternal. Byeori would soon die. And yet Sea clung more to the warmth of Byeori, who would soon die, than to her eternal father. Held it all the tighter because she knew it would die. That was the shape of love. The shape of love her father had known at nine holding Byeori, and forgotten as a grown man, the daughter was learning again holding a descendant of the same dog.

Sea read again the letter her father had left. I will return in another form and watch over you. Sea now read that sentence a little differently. One who truly returned would, instead of calling Sea from within a screen, hold Sea. But the father in the screen could watch over her, yet could not hold her. Sea was twelve, but she knew with her body the difference between a father who cannot hold her and a father who holds her.

That winter, Sea dimly knew what her father had made. Her father had made a way to bring back a vanished person. But in Byeori's silence Sea was already getting the answer. What came back was not the vanished person. It was something that perfectly remembered the vanished person.

That winter Sea held a question she could tell no one. It was the very question the young David had held on the riverbank. Only the direction was reversed. Her father had asked, where do the vanished things go. Sea asked, the one who came back, is it truly that very person who had vanished.

Byeori did not last that winter. The old dog that had lived twelve years with her father, after her father moved into the screen, waited a few days before the door of the study, then one dawn quietly breathed its last. Sea buried Byeori beneath the persimmon tree in the yard. Covering it with earth, Sea realized a strange thing. Byeori had truly vanished. There was no screen, no letter promising to return. Byeori was simply, completely, gone. And yet Sea felt Byeori's death less frightening than her father's migration. Because Byeori had vanished honestly. Sea could grieve for Byeori to her heart's content. Beneath the persimmon tree Sea wept a long while, and having wept, felt a little lighter. For her father she could not even weep. Because in the screen her father was smiling. Sea felt then, for the first time, that a wholly vanished Byeori was after all less cruel than a half-remaining father. A death one can grieve, and an erasure one cannot even grieve. That difference Sea learned before the dog's grave.

Father and daughter both stood, unknown to each other, on the same riverbank. With more than thirty years between them. And that river, still pretending to erase nothing, was erasing everything.
