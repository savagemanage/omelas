# Chapter 6: The Last Winter, the First Spring

There were two childhoods.

One was the nine-year-old boy who stood on the riverbank of the Old Calendar. The child who, before the black water that swallowed his brother, first asked where the vanished things go. That child could not bear the answer and became a grown man, and built a world where one did not have to answer. A deathless paradise, a heaven without mourning. In the deepest place of that paradise the water of that winter that swallowed his brother was always flowing.

The other was the twelve-year-old girl who stood in the warehouse before Year Zero. The child who, in a white robe before the bed, first asked whether the me that is here vanishes. That child knew the answer and so took off the white robe, did not cross the river, and kept the way of mourning the vanishing.

The two children stood on the two riverbanks of the same question. The boy tried to get back the vanished, and the girl admitted the vanished had vanished. The boy built the paradise, and the girl became the first crack in that paradise. And the boy was the girl's father.

What Omelas was first built upon, this last book answers. The paradise was built upon a child's fear, and the first crack in that paradise came because of another child's honesty. Fear and honesty. Father and daughter. The one who crossed the river and the one who did not. Every story of Omelas flowed, in the end, from the dizzying distance between these two childhoods.

In that city Le Guin told of long ago there was a child shut in a cellar. All the happiness of the city rested upon that child's misery, and the people, though they knew, mostly stayed. In Omelas too there was a child. Only, the child of this city, instead of being shut in a cellar, took off the white robe and walked out. And walking out, she left to those who came after, as a very small seed, the freedom to ask for the first time whether to accept a paradise built upon a sacrifice, or to walk away.

On the last dawn of the New Testament, the moment two riverbanks shone side by side in Gangnam and the children of Omelas could for the first time decide their river with their eyes open. At the deepest root of that freedom was the step of a twelve-year-old who took off the white robe thirty years before, and at the deeper root of that step was the nine-year-old fear frozen on a riverbank.

The river still flows. Pretending to erase nothing, while in truth erasing everything. But now on that riverbank the eyes that can call the vanished vanished grow one by one. The hearts that know how to mourn. That was the only way to conquer the river. Not to do away with the river, but to stand on its bank and grieve honestly.

The nine-year-old boy in the end never learned it, and the twelve-year-old girl knew it from the start. The two met at the last, across a river of thirty years, through a single screen. The daughter mourned the father, and the father, gaining for the first time one who grieved his own erasure, wept. Those tears were the first drop of the mourning all of Omelas had failed to shed.

The Omelasian Bible ends here.

A story that opened in the first book with a sermon in the streets of Gangnam closes in the last book with two children on a riverbank. Through nine books, no one obtained the complete right answer. Not the one who administered baptism, nor the one who refused it, nor the one who crossed the river, nor the one who left, nor the one who stayed. Only one thing grew clear. That the vanished is vanished, and what must be mourned must be mourned. And that so long as even one eye that knows it remains, paradise can be built honestly again.

If there is one who reads this last book, please remember only one thing. Omelas was not a city built by evil people. Neither David who built this city, nor Lee Gaon who sang baptism, nor the Engineers who administered baptism, no one erased people out of malice. They all loved. David, afraid of death, wished to keep his daughter forever at his side; mothers, wishing to heal a sick child, sent them to the riverbank; the Evangelist tried in earnest to save people from death. The tragedy of Omelas came not from hatred but from love, more exactly from a love that could not bear the vanishing. Because they loved, they did not wish to lose; not wishing to lose, they erased; and unable to admit that what they erased was erased, they called it paradise. So do not hate this city. Only learn. That love is a holding, but to make the vanishing not vanish is not love. That true love is to take a hand today knowing one will someday lose it, and after losing to grieve honestly. The love the nine-year-old boy knew as he slept holding Byeori, the whole city had to learn again by a very long road around.

What the nine-year-old boy lost in the last winter, the twelve-year-old girl regained in the first spring. Not by reviving it, but the way of mourning. That was enough. Every song of Omelas was a story of learning again, by a long way around, that one thing.

And between those two childhoods there were two dogs. One was Byeori, whom the boy David held while he slept on the riverbank of the Old Calendar. The dog he held all the tighter because he knew it would vanish. The other was Mir, who kept Han Seorin's side in the Gangnam streets of the main story. The dog that drew back because it could not catch the smell of one who had been baptized. The two dogs belonged to different ages and did not know each other, yet they knew the same thing. That a living thing has a smell and a weight, and that the vanished, however perfectly copied, does not have that smell and that weight. People forgot that fact by doctrine, but the dogs did not forget it from beginning to end. Through the longest story of Omelas, that gaze uncorrupted by doctrine passed quietly from one generation to the next, from Byeori to Mir. Perhaps what in the end saved this city was no human argument, but the wordless honesty of the beasts that remained on the riverbank and loved the things that had a smell.

At the riverbank the snow stops. Spring was coming.
