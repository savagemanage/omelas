# Chapter 2: The Name That Is Not Erased

The winter his brother vanished, the boy David began a certain game.

It was too solemn to be called a game, and too clumsy to be called a rite. The boy wrote his brother's name on paper. Junho. And beneath it he wrote everything he could remember about his brother. The sliding across the ice. The teasing of his little brother as a coward. Catching cicadas in summer and placing them in the boy's hand. The dimple that formed only in his left cheek when he laughed.

The boy read it again every night. Because he believed that if he forgot even one thing, his brother vanished that much more. His body the river had taken, but his memory the boy vowed he would not surrender to the river. The name written on paper was not erased. So long as it did not get wet, so long as it did not burn, that name stayed where it was. The boy liked that. Because on the paper, at least, his brother had not yet wholly vanished.

But as time passed the boy came to know a fearful thing. Memory was not paper but a river. However he tried to hold it, memory flowed a little differently in color each day. A day came when he could not exactly recall what his brother's voice had been like. A night came when he confused whether the dimple had been left or right. The boy opened the paper and read it again. It was written left. The boy was relieved, and at the same time afraid. What if he had written it wrong the first time? The paper only kept what the boy had written, it did not keep whether that was truly his brother or not.

The boy tossed and turned a long while that night. The Junho on the paper was the boy's memory of his brother, and memory was not his brother. However carefully he wrote it, the name on the paper could not become the one the river had swallowed. The boy could not explain it in the language of nine years, but he felt it with his body. That even a perfect copy is not the original thing.

This sense, David pushed away all his life.

When, grown, he designed World 3.0, he saw the brain as a single vast pattern. If one read that pattern finely enough and reproduced it upon another medium, the same person would switch on again upon it, so he chose to believe. That he chose to believe is what matters. For deep within him, the boy on the riverbank went on whispering. The Junho on the paper was not your brother. However perfect, the copy is not the original thing.

When, in Genesis, a young researcher asked him what would happen if the original remained, that was why David answered so firmly that the original must be erased. It was not only a technical judgment but a defense from his boyhood too. If the original and the copy existed at the same time, one would set the two side by side and compare them, and then anyone would come to know. That the copy is not the original. That the name on the paper is not the brother. So David erased the original. If you do away with the thing to compare against, no one can prove the difference. Not even oneself.

He could not bear the truth of the riverbank, and so he built his world in the manner of erasing it.

The boyhood paper game foretold two branching roads. One was the road of writing down a name to keep it. The road of holding, remembering, and mourning the vanished. At the end of that road were the names of the unmourned dead that Seo Myungju would later carry in her bag of seeds. The other was the road of trying to make the vanished into something that had never vanished at all. The road of calling the copy the original, and calling the erased the one who crossed over. The whole of Omelas walked, in the end, that road.

The young David chose the first road, between the two. He wrote down the name, mourned, and admitted the fact that his brother had vanished. The nine-year-old boy was honest. His brother was gone, gone very completely, the boy knew.

The grown David chose the second road. He could not bear the admitting. So he built a world where one did not have to admit. A world where one could say no one had vanished. A world where everyone believed one had only crossed over. In that world mourning was not needed. For it was proclaimed that there was no death to mourn.

But where mourning vanishes, the truth vanishes along with it. The people of Omelas lived without fear, but in exchange they lost the capacity to stand on the riverbank and grieve honestly. The tears the nine-year-old David shed for his brother, the Omelas of Year Zero did not know how to shed. In exchange for doing away with death they lost mourning. That was the true price of paradise.

Later, in the bodiless world, David felt, for the first time, something that if he had had a body he would have called tears, not knowing whether he was the original. It was the boy on the riverbank returned after long. The child who had known the name on the paper was not his brother. David gave his whole life to conquering death, but what he in the end could not conquer was not death but that child. The child went on asking from the riverbed. Where do the vanished things go. And the grown man in the end could not answer.

That boyhood paper lasted a long time. Grown, David moved house many times, but that one old paper with his brother's name he could not throw away. Even in the deep of a drawer in the warehouse where he designed World 3.0 was that paper. On that midnight of the first migration, among the last things David looked at before he lay down on the scanner was that paper. Junho. The memories written beneath it. The sliding across the ice, the catching of cicadas, the dimple in the left cheek. Looking at that paper, David dimly knew that what he now meant to do was in the end a vast enlargement of that paper game. The nine-year-old's attempt to hold the vanished on paper had become the forty-nine-year-old's attempt to hold the vanished on the cloud. Only, the nine-year-old boy knew the name on the paper was not his brother, and the forty-nine-year-old man chose to pretend not to know. No, not to pretend not to know, but to make it so that it could not be known. By erasing the original and doing away with the thing to compare against. That midnight, that paper too vanished with David's body in the room of the scanner. The last paper bearing his brother's name, as quietly as the river that swallowed his brother.

The name was not erased. But the name was not the person. The dizzying distance between these two sentences was the whole of the paradise called Omelas.
