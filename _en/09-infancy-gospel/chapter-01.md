# Chapter 1: The Boy and the River

Genesis wrote that David first felt death at forty-nine. That was true. But it was half the truth.

The fear was not born that winter on the floor of a meeting room. It had been growing quietly within him since far earlier, since a certain winter when a boy stood on a riverbank. Only, the grown David had chosen to forget that winter. Some memories are too heavy, and a person sinks them to the riverbed and walks over them as if nothing had happened.

The boy David was nine years old.

The place he grew up was the Gangnam before it was called Gangnam. A time when there were not yet glass towers or a lattice covering the sky. Low houses stood in a row along the river, and in winter a thin sheet of ice froze along the bank. The boy loved that river. The river seemed to erase nothing. A stone the boy threw, even after it left ripples, seemed to remain somewhere on the riverbed just as it was.

The boy had an older brother. His name was Junho. Three years older, and in the world the boy knew the biggest and most fearless person there was. The brother was not afraid of the river. When the ice froze he was the first up onto the river, and the boy stood on the bank and watched with envious eyes as his brother slid across the ice.

"Why don't you come up?" the brother asked from the ice.

"I'm scared," the boy said.

"Scared of what?"

The boy was a child who saw fearful things better than others. When other children looked at the ice and thought of a playground, the boy thought of the water flowing beneath it. When other children looked at the river and thought of summer bathing, the boy thought of how much the river must have carried away. The grown-ups called such a boy timid. But the boy's fear was no simple timidity. It was the fear of one who had learned earlier than others that everything in the world flows away and vanishes. The moment the boy came to like something, he began at once to worry about losing it. Looking at a new toy, he thought of how someday it would break; looking at a flower bloomed in spring, he thought of how it would wither by summer. To such a child the world was full of vanishing things. And his brother was what the boy most did not want to lose.

The boy could not answer. Only, the black water flowing beneath the ice, and the fact that no one knew where that water had come from or where it would go, held the boy to the bank. His brother laughed and went farther out on the ice. The boy would remember that laugh a long time. For it was his brother's last laugh.

The ice broke in an instant. The sound of thin glass cracking, and then a low sound of water swallowing something. The brother did not scream. He only looked once at the boy with startled eyes, and those eyes seemed about to say something, and then the black water took his brother away. The river seemed to erase nothing, but that was a lie. The river erased his brother. Without leaving even a ripple for long.

The boy stood frozen on the bank. Fear turned him to stone. He only stood, and watched the ice begin to freeze again over the place where his brother had vanished. The water soon grew still. As if nothing at all had happened. That was the part that tormented the boy the longest. That so great a thing had happened, and the river was not changed in the slightest.

The grown-ups came. When they found his brother downstream three days later, the brother was no longer his brother. The face was his brother's, but what made his brother his brother was not within it. The fearless laughing, the sliding across the ice, the teasing of his little brother. Where all of that had gone no one told him. The grown-ups said his brother had gone to a good place. The boy did not believe those words.

The night the funeral ended, the boy met, for the first time, the question. The question that would rule his whole life afterward, and at last come to cover the whole sky of Omelas.

Where do the vanished things go.

His brother's body was here. But his brother was gone. Then what made his brother his brother was not the body. Something other than the body, something held within the body that vanishes along with the body when the body stops. The boy could not bear that it had no name. For without a name one cannot grasp it, and what one cannot grasp one cannot get back.

The boy asked his mother. "Is my brother really gone? Completely, entirely?"

His mother held the boy a long while. She did not answer. That silence was the answer. The grown-ups did not know either. No one in the world knew where the vanished things go, and they feared to admit before a child that they did not know. That night the boy resolved, with a nine-year-old's heart, that if no one could answer the question, he would make an answer himself. How arrogant that resolve was, and how dangerous, the boy did not know.

After his brother vanished, a dog came to the boy's side. A puppy his father had brought from somewhere. The boy gave the dog the name Byeori. The reason was that there had been many stars in the night sky his brother looked up at from the ice. The boy slept holding Byeori. At night the black water that swallowed his brother came into his dreams, and each time the boy woke feeling with his palm the beating of Byeori's heart. The pulse of a living thing. That alone lifted the boy out of the dream of the black water. The boy knew that Byeori would someday grow old and die, and even the thought of it he already could not bear. So the boy held Byeori all the tighter. To hold all the tighter because one knows it will vanish, that was the shape of the love the boy first learned at nine. Later, grown, he would forget this love, and try to love only what does not vanish. But that what does not vanish cannot be loved, he should already have known that winter he slept holding Byeori.

After that the boy was changed. He saw the world with eyes that had learned everything in the world could vanish. He counted the wrinkles on his mother's face, watched the white fur spread over old Byeori, watched an old photograph fade in color. Everything was flowing toward the river. Toward that river which, while pretending to erase nothing, in truth erased everything.

The boy resolved to make things that do not vanish. That was why he became an Engineer. Bridges that do not collapse, networks that are not cut off, storehouses that do not lose what is kept. The work of letting nothing in the world vanish. The grown David called it a profession, but at its root was the nine-year-old boy frozen on the riverbank. The boy who vowed to the river that swallowed his brother that someday he would give it nothing.

But that boy did not know one thing. That making a thing not vanish and getting back a vanished thing are different. That to pretend to have gotten back what cannot be gotten back erases, more quietly and in greater measure than the river.

That river of Gangnam was later paved over, and roads were laid upon it, and glass towers rose, and at last a lattice covered the sky. No one remembered that a river had flowed beneath. But David remembered while having chosen to forget. In the deepest place of the deathless world he built, the black water of that winter was always flowing.
