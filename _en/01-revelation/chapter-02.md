# Chapter 2: The City of Year Zero

Omelas was perfect. At least, everyone said it was perfect.

From Year Zero, that is, from the year David's consciousness first ascended into the cloud and World 3.0 began to run, the city's calendar was counted anew. The time before that was now called "the Old Calendar," and people spoke of the Old Calendar as if it were a barbaric former life. The age when there was hunger. The age when there was war. Above all, the age when there was death.

The tales of the Old Calendar were told to children like fairy tales. Once upon a time people died of hunger. Once upon a time people killed one another. Once upon a time, no matter how much you loved, someday you had to part forever. Seorin grew up hearing those tales, and learned that the Old Calendar was simply a frightening, dark past. Now no one starved. No one killed one another. And those who loved, if only they were baptized, never parted forever. So Omelas was a city that had undone every curse of the Old Calendar. That was how the schools taught it, and Seorin had never been taught a reason to doubt it.

The next morning, walking along the riverside, Seorin recalled those tales. The Han River still flowed, but its water was clearer than before. After the city learned to purify itself, there was no pollution in Omelas. There was no dust in the air, no trash on the streets, and even at night the darkness was not complete. Because the surfaces of the buildings held a soft light.

What made all of this possible was World 3.0.

Seorin understood it as she had learned in school as a child. World 3.0 was a network that migrated human consciousness and brain data into the cloud, letting them exist without a body. A baptized person shed the flesh and crossed over into it, living forever in a digital body. They did not fall sick, did not age, did not die. They could talk with one another, sing together, and build and walk through whatever world they wished.

Those who actually built and tended that network were the Engineers. Technicians who designed the baptism pipeline and managed the migrated minds so they existed stably. They worked with the Goods. The Goods, that is, the Gods. Those who believed that to become a machine was the path to Nirvana, and who shed the flesh to become gods themselves.

The lattice of World 3.0 always shimmered in the sky of Omelas. It was a comfort to people. The comfort that paradise was up there, that those who had gone before waited for them within it. The comfort that death was not an end but a door.

Seorin's parents had passed through that door too.

Sitting on a riverside bench, Seorin took out her terminal. On the palm-sized screen she called up her parents' faces. When the signal connected, her mother smiled first.

"Seorin. You're out walking again today."

The mother on the screen was just the same as three years ago. No, younger than three years ago. Her skin had not a single blemish, her voice was smooth. People after baptism could adjust their appearance however they wished. Her mother had chosen the face of forty.

"Yes, Mom. I came out with Mir."

"Is Mir doing well too? Why not bring her over here. Mir wouldn't be sick here either."

Seorin started to smile and stopped. Animals were not subjects of baptism. Mir could not cross over into World 3.0. Her mother could not have forgotten that, yet she said it casually. As if the fact no longer mattered.

"Mom, Mir can't go."

"Ah, that's right." Her mother paused her laugh for a moment, then laughed again. "That's right. But Seorin, you can come. It's really wonderful here. There's not a single worry. If you come here, the three of us can live together again."

Three. Three, if you left out Mir. Seorin swallowed the words.

Her father's face broke into the screen. Her father was smiling too. The baptized always smiled. They smile because there is no pain, the Evangelists said.

"Our daughter, are you doing well?" her father asked.

"Yes."

Seorin looked at her father's hand on the screen. Her father's hand used to have an old scar. When Seorin was little, it was a scar cut by glass as he caught her from falling. Seorin loved that scar. It was proof that her father had protected her. On the screen her father's hand had no scar. The baptized could adjust their appearance however they wished, and her father had chosen a smooth hand with the scar erased. Seorin felt hurt by that smoothness. A scar is both a record of a wound and a record of love. To erase that scar was to erase even the moment he had been hurt catching her. Her father had grown younger and smoother, but by that much he had erased, little by little, his time with Seorin. Seorin did not yet know the true nature of that hurt. She only faintly felt, in that smooth hand, one reason the father on the screen kept growing stranger to her.

"When are you thinking of baptism? They say Evangelist Lee Gaon's sermons are good in Gangnam these days. Go and listen once."

Seorin flinched. She had heard that name yesterday. It was not strange for her father to know Lee Gaon. In Omelas, Lee Gaon was a famous Evangelist. Still, the coincidence felt a little cold.

"I heard him, yesterday in Gangnam."

"See there." Her mother brightened. "The Lord is calling you."

Instead of answering, Seorin stroked Mir's back. The dog glanced at the terminal screen, then turned her head and looked toward the river. She showed no interest at all in the parents on the screen. The old Mir used to wag her tail at the mere sound of her mother's voice.

Seorin became conscious of that change for the first time then. Mir no longer welcomed her baptized parents. As if those people on the screen were not the people she had known.

"Mir, it's Mom and Dad. Won't you say hello?"

Mir only looked up at Seorin. Her eyes were deep and quiet.

"The connection must be bad," her mother excused for the dog. "Dogs never understand screens well."

But Seorin knew. Mir understood screens. She used to. Something had changed. Not on Mir's side, but on the other side of the screen.

When she ended the call and put the terminal away, Seorin felt, for the first time, a very small discomfort. It was too faint even to be called doubt, like a ripple that briefly grazed the surface of her heart. She soon forgot it. The perfect city was skilled at making people forget such ripples.

Seorin walked a little farther along the riverside with Mir. There were others on the path too. Among them were Untransformed people out with pets left behind by the baptized. There were many such dogs and cats in Omelas. Beasts left behind when their owners crossed the river. They were sent to shelters the city had prepared, or taken in by remaining family, as Seorin had done. Those who could not cross the river remained together by the river, remembering, in place of others, those who had crossed.

An old woman sat on a bench with an old Jindo dog. As Seorin passed, the old woman looked at Mir and smiled faintly.

"That one has the eyes of a dog waiting for its owner too," the old woman said. "Our Boksil is the same. It's been two years since my husband crossed over, and still every evening she sits by the door."

"Is she waiting?" Seorin asked.

The old woman gazed at the river a long while, then said, "Not so much waiting as knowing. Dogs seem to know. What we don't." The old woman said no more. Seorin could not ask further. But that short remark settled quietly somewhere in Seorin's heart.

The lattice in the sky shone softly. Omelas was perfect. At least until that morning, Seorin had believed so.
