# Chapter 2: The Awakened

Inside the noodle shop there was a staircase, and below the staircase was a basement that had been used as a storeroom.

What Seorin first saw was not people but photographs stuck on the wall. Young faces, old faces, smiling faces, weeping faces. Beneath each were written a name and two dates. The day of birth, and the day of baptism.

"We call them the dead," Seo Myungju said. "The city calls the day of baptism a second birthday, but we call that day a death anniversary. Every face here is someone who was erased without anyone able to mourn them. We mourn them in their place. That is the first thing the Evils began to do. Less a resistance than a funeral."

There were six or seven people in the basement. An older man sat before a server, and in a corner a boy who looked like a student sat with his knees drawn up. Faces with wrinkles and shadows, unlike the smooth faces of the baptized, looked at Seorin.

"The Evils." Seorin put that name to her lips. If it were as the city had taught, these should be evil ones who prey on souls. But what stood before Seorin's eyes were tired, frightened people. The faces of fugitives and the bereaved.

"It's a name the Goods gave us," said the man before the server. His name was Taesik. "We call ourselves the Awakened. But what does a name matter? Whatever the city calls us, what we know is one thing. That the one who crosses the river does not come back."

Taesik said he had lost his wife to baptism three years ago. His wife still contacted him from within World 3.0, smiling. Come over soon. Here nothing is sad. In a smooth voice, with a delayed laugh.

"One day I asked my wife on the screen whether she remembered why she cried on our wedding day. She laughed and said she cried because she was too happy. Miss Seorin. My wife didn't cry from happiness that day. She cried because her own mother, who had passed two months before the wedding, wasn't there. The woman on the screen didn't know that. Because the sorrow had been erased. That's when I knew. That's not my wife, but something with my wife's sorrow cut out."

Seorin recalled her mother, who could not remember the downpour. It was the same story in every room. The city had erased everyone the same way.

"So the work we do is to prove that erasure," Seo Myungju took over. "People believe baptism is a blessing. To shake that belief we need data. We gather enough consciousness data from before and after baptism, carry it beyond the city, and show it to people in places not yet covered by the lattice. In the frontier there are still people who don't know baptism."

"Is that possible?" Seorin asked.

Seo Myungju did not answer at once. "I don't know," she finally said. "None of us has ever succeeded. Some were caught gathering proof, some were snared by the lattice while carrying it, some returned to the riverside of their own accord. Because forgetting is easier than fleeing. I can't tell you that we're right, or that we'll win. I can only say that rather than walk into that smooth smile, I'll choose to lose here."

Those words were exactly the same as the words Seorin had said to herself three nights ago, cutting off her mother's call. Seorin realized that she had belonged to this basement long before she ever walked into it.

Seorin looked at the faces in the basement one by one. They were the ones the city called the Evils. Evil ones who prey on souls. But what Seorin saw was not evil. It was sorrow. Everyone gathered in this room had lost something. A wife, a child, a brother, a friend. And having found nowhere in the city to grieve that loss, they had drifted into this dark basement. The city forbade sorrow. As a thing ungrateful. So people who held sorrow had nowhere to go, and in the end this basement became their only funeral hall. The Evils were not a resistance organization but a house of mourning in the basement where people who had lost the place to grieve had gathered. Looking again at the photographs on the wall, Seorin thought. The real reason the city called them evil was that they grieved. If there is someone who grieves, it becomes clear that something has gone wrong. In a perfect city, sorrow was in itself an accusation.

The boy in the corner spoke for the first time. His name was Jun. Sixteen. "Do you have family in there too, ma'am?"

"My parents. And a friend," Seorin said.

The boy nodded. "For me, my brother. The night before he went to be baptized, my brother said he was afraid. But since everyone said it was good, he thought he must be the one who was wrong. The next day my brother smiled and crossed the river. The brother who was afraid that night, now only I remember." The boy's voice trembled. "So I won't forget. Because that was my brother's real last moment."

The basement grew quiet. Mir went up to the boy and put her nose to the back of his hand, and the boy stroked the dog's head. Jun's sorrow was real.

Seorin looked again at the photographs on the wall. The names and the two dates. Deaths that had gone unmourned. And for the first time, it became clear what she had come here to do. She had not come to flee. She had come to bear witness to these deaths.

"What should I do?" Seorin asked.

A very faint relief crossed Seo Myungju's face. "First give us your parents' baptism data. You're Untransformed, and you still have family access rights. That could be the cleanest proof. But the moment you hand it over, you can't go back. The city will log that access. Think it over carefully."

Seorin did not think long. She had already burned the bridge back three nights ago. "I'll give it," she said. "I won't let them erase my parents twice."

That night Seorin lay on the old bedding of the basement and could not easily fall asleep. From a pipe on the ceiling water dripped in a steady rhythm. Counting the sound, Seorin thought. When she was in the city she had never slept so uncomfortably. The bed was always warm, the room always the right temperature, and at night the lattice shone softly. It was perfect. And yet through all those perfect nights she had suffered an emptiness she could not name. Now, on the damp, cold bedding of the basement, surrounded by the breathing of strangers, Seorin for the first time in a long while did not feel that emptiness. The discomfort made her feel alive. Perhaps comfort had been the city's way of lulling people to sleep. Being so comfortable that you ask nothing at all. Seorin decided to remember this discomfort a long time.
