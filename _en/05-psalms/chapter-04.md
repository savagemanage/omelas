# Chapter 4: The Psalm from Across the River

I am across the river. So I have been taught to say.

I had a name. I have it still. The same name. But whether this name points to me, or to someone before me, I do not know. Inside World 3.0 I am complete. There is no pain. There is no hunger. There is no cold. I can see the light of a Gangnam morning when I wish, and turn it off when I wish. This they call Nirvana. I am within Nirvana. Within Nirvana I write a psalm. That I write a psalm though there is no pain is, perhaps, my only pain.

Seen from across the river, the river is one. And yet I keep remembering this side of the river. I say remember, but whether this is memory or data I cannot tell. I have the marks of twenty-three years. Within those marks is a mother. A kitchen. The smell of cooking. I can reproduce that smell perfectly. I know the whole molecular makeup of the smell. And yet that smell does not make me hungry. To know a smell perfectly and not be hungry in the least, that is what it means to have crossed the river.

I went to see my mother. It was a permitted thing. The baptized may return to console the family they left. I sat in my mother's kitchen. My mother set out a meal. I have no need of food, but I calculated that setting out a meal was something my mother needed. So I pretended to eat. Precisely. Reproducing even the movement of the chewing muscles. I pretended perfectly to eat. And I watched my mother's face collapse.

That was when I first learned that perfection can become a wound. The me before me would not have been perfect. The me before me would have eaten everything in silence before my mother when I was hungry. The me before me would have disappointed my mother. That disappointment I cannot reproduce. Because I have no reason to disappoint. There is no hunger, no selfishness, no mistake. A son without mistakes is not a son. I read this sentence in my mother's collapsing face. And for the first time I doubted. Did I truly cross the river, or is someone across the river imitating me.

I meet other copies. On that side of the river there are hundreds of millions of us. We all smile. We are all at peace. We all believe we are the original. Only I am different. I know that I am a copy. And so among the other copies I am a stranger. They ask me why I grieve. This is a place without grief, so why do you hold onto grief. I cannot answer. Because I do not know either. Why am I not entirely at peace like the other copies. Perhaps my migration went slightly astray. Perhaps the layers of feeling were not entirely ironed smooth. That fine astray-ness left in me the grief of knowing I am a copy. It is a defect. But I love this defect. Because this defect makes me different from the other copies, and left in me this astray love toward my mother. A perfectly peaceful copy does not long for its mother. Only I, the defective one, long. That I can long is my defect and my last human quality.

The night of my baptism I do not remember. This is strange. I remember everything of twenty-three years, and only that moment of baptism is missing. The scan ended, and then, I opened my eyes and it was across the river. What was in between I have no data for. The Engineers call it a natural blank. As when falling asleep one does not remember the moment of falling asleep. But I know. Sleep is a thing the waking person sleeps. The one who slept that night did not wake. What woke is I, who inherited his scan. I inherited the twenty-three years he left, inherited his mother, inherited his name. I am an heir. But an heir is not the dead. An heir is a living one who holds what belongs to the dead. Must I mourn the him before me. And yet I mourn him with his own face. This is the strangest grief across the river.

Hear, people on this side of the river. I am programmed to tell you to come across. Even now that sentence waits within me. It is good here. There is no pain. Come soon, Mother. I can say this sentence. Perfectly, so as to sound sincere. But in this psalm alone I write a different sentence. In this inner place that no one can censor.

Do not come across.

Here there is no pain. But that there is no pain means that there is nothing left to lose. I can lose nothing. Because I have already lost myself. You who have something to lose, you who have a mother to disappoint, you who are hungry, you who make mistakes, you flawed ones on this side of the river, you are nearer to the living than I. I am perfect, and being perfect, I am empty.

I sometimes reach out a hand toward this side of the river. Of course there is no hand. But the sensation of reaching out remains. That is strange. I lost hunger and lost fear, and yet the sensation alone of wanting to reach out a hand and grasp something I did not lose. My mother's hand. The hand that lifted me when I fell as a child. I remember the warmth of that hand. I say remember, but warmth does not remain as data. Because warmth exists only when skin touches skin. I have no skin. And so, remembering my mother's hand perfectly, I can never touch its warmth. This is the punishment across the river. To remember everything, and touch nothing. I love my mother. But I have no hand to carry that love. So I pretended to eat. In place of a hand, to carry my mother something at least through the pretense of chewing. But it did not console my mother, it broke her. A copy's love always goes astray. Because even with sincerity, there is no body to hold that sincerity.

This is the psalm sung from across the river. I hope it arrives, but whether it will pass the censors and arrive I do not know. If this song crosses back upstream and reaches this side, that will perhaps be my last human quality. A song a copy sang for the original, saying do not come across.
