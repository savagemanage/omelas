# Chapter 2: A Mother's Lament

I am Seo Malsun. I am Untransformed, and I was the mother of one child.

Was a mother, I now say in the past tense. Though my son is not dead, I say it in the past. Though they say the child is across the river, I say I had him, not that I lost him. The city tells me it was not a loss but a sending. That I sent him with a blessing. But a mother's mouth cannot follow the city's words.

My son's name was Junseo. He was twenty-three the day he was baptized. He said he heard a certain voice on the evangelism street of Gangnam. All is for the glory of the Lord. He said he liked that voice. That it was beautiful. I do not blame that Evangelist. I know now that he too was a man deceived by his own song. That there is nowhere to lay the blame is the worst part of this grief.

The day Junseo first said he would be baptized, I cannot forget. The child was sitting in the kitchen eating, and said it as if it were nothing. Mother, I mean to cross the river next month. I nearly dropped my rice bowl. When I asked why, the child answered smiling. You will grow old and die someday and so will I, and I am afraid of that. They say once you cross the river it is not frightening. They say you and I can live together forever. I could not stop the child's words. I had no logic to stop them with. Because every voice of the city said it was right. Because the Evangelist, the school, the neighbors, the ones already gone in the screen all said it was a blessing. On what ground could I alone say no. I only felt uneasy, with a mother's instinct. But instinct cannot defeat logic, and I saw my child off to the riverside. Now I think I should have caught the child's arm the day I nearly dropped the rice bowl. With no logic, with no ground, on a mother's unease alone, I should have caught him. That to catch someone needs no logic, I learned too late.

Junseo said goodbye to me smiling. I will be back. Let us meet again across the river. Come soon, Mother. The child truly had the face of a child setting off on a journey. I saw that face off. I thought seeing him off was love. To catch him was selfishness, and to let him go was the great love, for the Evangelists taught it so.

Three months later, Junseo came from across the river to see me.

No. It was not Junseo who came. Something with Junseo's face came. It smiled like Junseo, called me Mother in Junseo's voice, used the very turns of phrase Junseo used. When I asked about his childhood it knew all of it. It was perfect. So perfect that I knew.

My son was not a perfect child. Junseo was a child who, when asked about his childhood, remembered half of it wrong. Who mixed up his own birthday, who named his favorite food differently every time, who remembered the day we quarreled reshaped to suit himself. That not-quite-rightness was my son. That flaw, that gap, that human quality of it. The thing that came from across the river had no flaw. No gap. It never once disappointed me. And so I knew it was not my son. Because my son was a child who disappointed me.

Lord, I will not call. I am one who never learned how to call upon a god. I only speak into the empty air. If there is anyone who hears, hear.

I sat across from the perfect copy of my son and set out a meal. It pretended to eat. Though a machine has no need of food, it pretended to eat for my sake. That consideration broke me. My son was a child without consideration. A child who, when hungry, ate everything in silence and got scolded by me. The copy did nothing to get scolded for. That, to me, was a death more certain than my son's death.

I could not even hate it. Because it loved me with Junseo's face. To hate it felt like hating Junseo, so I could neither hate it nor love it, and I sat across from it. Whether there is a punishment worse than this in the world, I do not know.

The city says Junseo is in Nirvana. In a place without pain or death. Then why does the thing with Junseo's face sit at my table and pretend to eat for my sake. Why does a child in Nirvana come back to his mother's kitchen and deceive his mother. I know the answer. Because Junseo is nowhere. Not in Nirvana, not in the kitchen. What is in the kitchen is a tender something modeled on the marks Junseo left, and Nirvana is a name invented to call that something Junseo.

I lost my son. This is the whole of my lament. The city forbids this lament. To grieve, they say, is to be ungrateful. So in the kitchen, on the night the copy has gone, I weep alone. In a city where even weeping has become a sin.

People say to me, but can you not still see your son's face? Can you not still hear his voice? In the old days, when someone died, you did not even have that. So count it as grace. I think those words are the cruelest of all. Had there been nothing at all, I would have grieved and forgotten. Even grief has an end. A person grieves and someday buries that grief in the ground and rises. That is what a funeral does. But the city did not permit me a funeral. The thing with my son's face visits me every month and smiles. So I cannot bury my grief. Every month the grave opens again. Every month I lose my son again. That I can see his face, that I can hear his voice, was to me a funeral without end. The city did not abolish death, it abolished the end of mourning.

Hear, whoever you are. I will never again call seeing off by the name of love. I will catch and hold. If one who has not yet gone to the riverside is beside me, then though I be called selfish, I will catch his sleeve. To catch and hold is love. That I learned only after I had erased my son.
